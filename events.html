From organizational history to professional practice

Internal communication has a longer and more complex history than a simple story of newsletters evolving into digital channels. The webinar draws on historical research tracing organized communication with employees from the late nineteenth century to the present. Its central argument is that internal communication has repeatedly changed in response to wider social, economic and management shifts. Organizations have used it to address practical challenges such as workforce stability, changing expectations of employees, organizational objectives and culture.

Read the full briefing: Internal Communication Professionalism

The historical account also challenges easy origin stories. For example, the presenter cautions against describing The Lowell Offering as the first company magazine: it was not produced by a company for its employees. The broader lesson is to define internal communication carefully and verify historical claims against evidence.

The discussion then turns to whether internal communication is a profession, or a field still undergoing professionalization. The speakers describe a substantial practitioner community, but point to comparatively low participation in professional bodies, formal qualifications and structured continuing development. They also identify a gap between what professional bodies define as good practice and what employers and recruiters know to look for.

What this means for practice

  • Understand the organizational need behind communication work and explain how the function contributes to it.
  • Treat professional development, shared standards and clearer recruitment expectations as part of strengthening the field.
  • Use history to question assumptions about the role, rather than treating current channels or titles as inevitable.

The webinar presents internal communication as both a response to organizational needs and a practice whose future depends on practitioners making its contribution understood.

Source: “History of Internal Communication and what it means for current and future practice” transcript.

What senior leaders expect

This session explores how senior managers understand the role of internal communication and what they expect it to contribute. The research discussed in the webinar highlights a function increasingly seen as strategic: helping people understand organizational direction, prepare for change, stay connected and contribute to shared objectives.

Read the full briefing: Perspectives and Challenges of Internal Communication

Several dimensions recur in leaders’ accounts: transparency, cohesion, proximity between leaders and employees, two-way communication, attention to workforce diversity, digital channels and employee wellbeing. In uncertain environments, clear and transparent communication can help people understand what is changing and why. Listening matters too: employee voice can strengthen belonging and help people feel ownership of organizational work.

The session also considers pressures reshaping the function, including digital transformation and AI, sustainability and ESG expectations, cultural change, multigenerational workforces, talent attraction and retention, and mental health. These demands make internal communication more than the production and distribution of messages.

The practitioner perspective emphasizes that leaders are not simply buying communication outputs; they need sound judgment and advice. Communication can influence decisions before they are made, but practitioners need to work with the leaders and relationships available to them. Trust and confidence are central, and communication problems can sometimes reflect leadership or operational issues that communication alone cannot fix.

What this means for practice

  • Connect communication plans to organizational decisions and outcomes, not just channel activity.
  • Make space for employee questions and feedback, then show how that input is handled.
  • Adapt messages and channels for different groups without losing transparency or consistency.
  • Use curiosity and advice to build influence; recognize when the underlying issue is not a communication output problem.

The findings reflect the research presented in the webinar and should be read in that context, rather than assumed to represent every senior leader or organization.

Listening across the organization

Internal social media can create a shared communication space where employees see one another’s contributions, respond to colleagues and build on ideas over time. The webinar illustrates how a discussion thread can surface a need that might otherwise stay local: employees exchange observations about products for younger customers, colleagues add further perspectives, and managers invite contributors to help develop a response. The example shows the potential of visible, persistent employee discussion to support listening and participation.

Read the full briefing: Transforming Internal Communication with Internal Social Media and AI

The academic presentation describes internal social media as one part of a broader listening architecture. Listening is not simply collecting comments: employees need respectful responses. A routine reply that redirects a concern into a process without addressing it can feel like “pseudo listening”; a response that acknowledges the concern and explains what is known can show respect even when there is no immediate solution.

The practitioner presentation demonstrates possible uses of AI with internal social media, including summarizing discussions, identifying themes, preparing executive briefs and informing change planning. These demonstrations used a constructed example thread, so they illustrate possible workflows rather than prove results from a live organization.

What this means for practice

  • Make employee participation easy and ensure contributions receive meaningful responses.
  • Use AI-generated summaries as prompts for investigation, not as a complete or neutral account of employee opinion. A small number of active posters may not represent everyone.
  • Compare platform signals with other evidence, such as surveys and direct conversations.
  • Establish clear expectations for privacy, trust, access and responsible AI use. The webinar raises ethical questions but does not examine them in depth.

The opportunity is to use digital discussion and AI to improve organizational understanding while keeping human judgment and respectful listening at the center.

Source: “Using AI and internal social media for dialogue” transcript.

How communication creates value

Drawing on a review of more than 60 research and industry reports, this webinar considers how internal communication creates value for organizations and employees. The evidence discussed links effective internal communication with organizational performance, alignment, culture, engagement and change. It can help people understand priorities, coordinate work, contribute to innovation and navigate change. It also shapes relationships, trust and employees’ experience of the organization.

Read the full briefing: Valuing Internal Communication

The session cautions against reducing value to the volume of content produced or to a single productivity measure. Communication contributes alongside leadership, operations and other organizational factors; it should not claim sole responsibility for outcomes. The practitioner discussion illustrates the value of connecting communication to decisions and measurable outcomes, such as whether a change is understood and implemented with fewer obstacles.

Planning, measurement and evaluation are recurring themes. Practitioners may need to look across several indicators and consider broader patterns rather than relying on one survey question or channel metric. The report also frames internal communication as relationship-building, not only channel management and content distribution.

What this means for practice

  • Start with the organizational or employee outcome communication is intended to support.
  • Explain the contribution of communication without overstating causality.
  • Evaluate a combination of relevant indicators, including understanding, behavior, employee experience and operational outcomes where appropriate.
  • Include employee value, such as feeling informed, engaged and empowered, alongside organizational value.

The webinar’s message is that value is strongest when communication is planned around a real purpose and evaluated with evidence, rather than assumed from activity alone.

Appreciation as part of organizational culture

This webinar brings together research and practitioner experience to examine how appreciation is experienced at work and how organizations can make it part of everyday culture. Appreciation is not limited to formal awards or praise from senior leaders. It can come from managers, colleagues, organizational leaders and customers, and can be communicated through recognition, respect, trust, meaningful work and opportunities to contribute.

Read the full briefing: Developing an Appreciative Working Climate

The research presented includes a survey of employees working in Germany. On a seven-point scale, 55.6% of respondents rated appreciation from their manager at the top end of the scale, and 58.8% did so for appreciation from colleagues. The presenters also note that more than 40% did not regularly receive appreciation from a manager or coworker. These findings describe the surveyed group, not all employees.

The session reports that manager appreciation is associated with job satisfaction and some forms of contribution beyond formal duties; appreciation from coworkers is associated with organizational identification and helping colleagues. Employees also value some contributions, such as willingness to take responsibility, more than they feel those contributions are recognized. Appreciation therefore needs to be sincere and aligned with what employees actually value.

What this means for practice

  • Make recognition part of regular routines: feedback conversations, team meetings and acknowledgement of everyday contributions.
  • Equip managers to listen, show respect and recognize contributions fairly.
  • Reinforce appreciation through organizational stories, structures and reward practices, not campaigns alone.
  • Address working conditions and power dynamics that prevent people from contributing or feeling seen.

Using a cultural framework, the webinar argues that an appreciative climate is built through repeated practices and organizational choices. Communication can support that work, but cannot substitute for it.

The work happening between formal messages

Informal communication includes spontaneous conversations, quick exchanges about work, social connection and digital chats. It is often treated as peripheral to internal communication, yet it can support coordination, relationships and employees’ sense of belonging. The webinar combines research with practitioner experience to challenge the assumption that informal talk is mainly gossip or unrelated to work.

Read the full briefing: Informal Internal Communication

The academic study surveyed employees working in Germany and used factor analysis to group nine measured functions into four core functions: information and coordination; belonging and connectedness; stress management and recreation; and building personal relationships. All four were considered relevant, with information and coordination the most pivotal. The researchers report differences across groups: for example, personal relationships were more important to remote workers, while belonging and recreation were more important to younger respondents. These are findings from the study’s sample, not universal rules.

Practitioner examples show how informal interaction can help leaders understand how messages are received and help colleagues solve problems. In hybrid and distributed teams, some of these opportunities may need deliberate support, such as creating time and spaces for casual exchange.

What this means for practice

  • Treat informal communication as a useful complement to formal channels and a possible signal of employee concerns.
  • Encourage opportunities for connection, especially for remote or dispersed colleagues.
  • Listen with care, but do not try to control ordinary employee conversation.
  • Use informal signals alongside other evidence; one conversation or visible online discussion cannot represent the whole workforce.

The session’s central challenge for communicators is to make room for informal connection and learn from it without turning it into another managed broadcast channel.
